1. Case Selection & Confidentiality

  • Clinical Context: ICU / Neuro-Critical Care / Emergency & Trauma Nursing.
  • Case Overview: A post-operative patient status-post decompressive craniectomy for an acute ischemic stroke (AIS) who experienced acute neurological deterioration and required urgent hemodynamic, airway/perfusion, and pharmacological management.
  • Ethical & Confidentiality Compliance: All patient identifiers (name, hospital number, exact date of birth) have been omitted to maintain strict patient confidentiality and ethical standards.

2. Brief Patient Summary

  • Demographics: 59-year-old male.
  • Medical Diagnosis: Acute Ischemic Stroke (AIS) post-decompressive craniectomy.
  • Chief Complaint / Acute Event: Sudden decrease in level of consciousness (LOC), left-sided hemiplegia, facial droop, and dysarthria occurring post-operatively while under critical care monitoring.
  • Mode of Arrival: Via Ambulance to ER, then transferred to the Neuro-ICU via emergency surgical bed following urgent decompressive craniectomy after emergency department stabilization and neuroimaging.

Initial Vital Signs (at acute neurological change)

BP 178/96 mmHg
HR 62 bpm
RR 18 breaths/min
SpO₂ 98% (2L NC)
Temp 37.8 °C

Primary Survey Findings (ABCDE):

A (Airway): Patent, maintained independently with intact gag/cough reflex, though at risk due to declining consciousness and post-stroke dysphagia.

B (Breathing): Spontaneous, unlabored respirations at 18/min; bilateral equal chest expansion; clear lung sounds; SpO₂ 98% on low-flow oxygen.

C (Circulation): Hemodynamically hypertensive (178/96 mmHg) with borderline bradycardia (62 bpm — early warning sign of rising intracranial pressure / Cushing's response); peripheral pulses strong and regular; two large-bore IV cannulas patent.

D (Disability): GCS 10/15 (E3, V2, M5); pupils unequal and sluggishly reactive (Right 3.5 mm, Left 3 mm); left-sided motor deficit (0/5 strength in left upper and lower extremities); blood glucose 132 mg/dL.

E (Exposure): Decompressive craniectomy surgical dressing clean, dry, and intact; subgaleal surgical drain in place draining serosanguinous fluid; no external bleeding or skin breakdown.

3. Chronological Clinical Scenario (Mini Clinical Narrative)

08:00 — Baseline Assessment: Assumed care of the 59-year-old male in the critical care unit following decompressive craniectomy for a massive middle cerebral artery (MCA) ischemic stroke. The patient was initially drowsy but arousable (GCS 13/15), hemodynamically stable, and receiving scheduled intravenous antimicrobial and antipyretic therapy.

10:15 — Acute Deterioration: During hourly neurological checks, the patient became increasingly lethargic (GCS dropped to 10/15), with new-onset left facial asymmetry and sluggish pupillary light response. Systolic blood pressure rose sharply to 178 mmHg while heart rate trended down to 62 bpm.

10:20 — Immediate Nursing Stabilization & Team Escalation: Performed an immediate ABCDE reassessment, elevated the head of the bed (HOB) to 30 degrees with the head aligned in a neutral midline position to optimize cerebral venous drainage, and notified the neurosurgical and critical care medical team immediately.

10:30 — Diagnostic & Therapeutic Interventions: Accompanied the patient for an emergent non-contrast head CT and CT angiography to rule out post-craniectomy hematoma or hemorrhagic transformation. Because recent cranial surgery is an absolute contraindication to systemic thrombolysis (tPA), care focused on strict blood pressure titration (target SBP 140–180 mmHg via ordered IV antihypertensives) to preserve collateral perfusion to the ischemic penumbra without provoking surgical site bleeding.

15:00–17:00 — Ongoing Pharmacological & Neuro-Protective Management: Administered prescribed medications safely after verifying allergy status, renal/hepatic baseline labs, and swallowing safety:

  • 15:00: Ceftazidime 2g IV for post-neurosurgical infection prophylaxis/treatment.
  • 17:00: Paracetamol 1g IV to maintain normothermia and prevent fever-induced metabolic cerebral stress, alongside Azithromycin 500mg oral/enteral after confirming safe swallowing/tube placement.

18:30 — Stabilization: Following targeted hemodynamic control, normothermia maintenance, and close neuro-monitoring, the patient's GCS stabilized at 13/15, pupils returned to equal and briskly reactive (3 mm), and follow-up imaging confirmed absence of intracranial hemorrhage.

4. Clinical Problem & Learning Focus (Challenges During Practice)

  • Managing a Rapidly Deteriorating Neuro-Critical Patient: Recognizing subtle early indicators of secondary brain injury (a 3-point GCS drop paired with rising systolic blood pressure and falling heart rate) before irreversible brain herniation occurred.
  • Balancing Competing Hemodynamic Goals: Navigating the clinical challenge of maintaining sufficient cerebral perfusion pressure (CPP) to salvage ischemic brain tissue while simultaneously preventing severe hypertension that could cause life-threatening hemorrhage at the fresh craniectomy site.
  • Medication Safety in Dysphagic & Post-Surgical Patients: Ensuring safe administration of both IV and oral medications (evaluating aspiration risk prior to oral Azithromycin and monitoring hepatotoxicity/nephrotoxicity thresholds) under high-acuity time constraints.
  • Communication Under Pressure: Delivering concise, structured SBAR updates to the multidisciplinary neuro-team during an acute neurological emergency.

Ethical Dilemmas (Informed Consent, Impaired Capacity & Family Presence):

  • Impaired Decision-Making Capacity & Emergency Consent: Because the patient experienced an acute drop in consciousness (GCS 10/15) and expressive dysarthria, he lacked the capacity to provide informed consent for emergent diagnostic imaging (CT/CTA) or potential endovascular interventions. Balancing the ethical principle of autonomy against beneficence (acting immediately to save brain tissue) required relying on emergency implied consent protocols and swiftly contacting the patient's legal surrogate/next-of-kin.
  • Managing Family Presence & Psychological Distress: Balancing the family's emotional need to remain at the bedside during a sudden neurological decline with the clinical team's need for rapid, unobstructed access to stabilize the patient and prepare for emergency transport, while providing honest, compassionate, and non-alarming updates.

5. Application of a Reflection Framework (Gibbs' Structured Clinical Reflection)

Description of the Event: Caring for a post-craniectomy stroke patient who suddenly deteriorated neurologically required rapid coordination between bedside nursing stabilization, emergency neuroimaging, and precise medication administration.
Analysis — What Went Well and Why:
  • Frequent, systematic neurological checks (GCS, pupillary reactivity, motor strength) enabled early detection of deterioration before respiratory compromise developed.
  • Immediate non-pharmacological neuro-protective positioning (30° HOB elevation and midline neck alignment) and thorough pre-medication safety checks (allergy verification, dysphagia awareness, fever control) directly supported patient safety.
Analysis — What Could Have Been Improved:
  • During the acute spike in blood pressure, there was a brief delay in preparing the vasoactive infusion pump while awaiting imaging confirmation, increasing cognitive load and stress during urgent transport.
  • Swallowing assessment documentation for oral medications could have been verified earlier in the shift prior to scheduled medication times.
Future Action Plan & Skill Development:
  • What I will do differently: Proactively prepare emergency vasoactive infusions and airway/suction equipment at the bedside of high-risk post-craniectomy patients at the start of every shift.
  • Skills & knowledge to strengthen: Enhance proficiency in calculating Cerebral Perfusion Pressure (CPP = MAP − ICP), interpreting early Cushing's triad trends, and performing standardized bedside dysphagia screens.
  • Helpful training/simulation: Participating in high-fidelity neuro-emergency simulations focusing on post-craniotomy neurological decline and rapid SBAR escalation.

How This Experience Will Change My Future Practice:

This experience has shifted my clinical mindset from reactive task execution to proactive critical anticipation. In my future emergency and critical care nursing practice, I will treat subtle neurological changes—such as a 2-to-3-point GCS decline or early widening pulse pressure—as immediate triggers for rapid escalation rather than isolated findings. Furthermore, I will integrate early family engagement and surrogate decision-maker verification at admission so that ethical consent processes and compassionate family support remain seamless during sudden, life-threatening deteriorations.

6. Integration of Evidence-Based Practice (EBP)

American Heart Association / American Stroke Association (AHA/ASA) Guidelines: Confirm that recent intracranial surgery within 3 months is an absolute contraindication to intravenous fibrinolysis (Alteplase/Tenecteplase), making blood pressure control, normothermia, normoglycemia, and endovascular evaluation the cornerstones of acute ischemic stroke care.
Brain Trauma & Neurocritical Care Standards: Maintaining HOB elevation at 30 degrees with neutral head alignment promotes jugular venous outflow and lowers intracranial pressure (ICP) without impairing cerebral blood flow. Additionally, aggressive fever control (using IV Paracetamol to keep temperature <37.5°C) reduces cerebral metabolic oxygen demand (CMRO₂) and limits secondary neuronal injury.
Hospital Medication & Stroke Protocols: Performing formal dysphagia screening prior to administering oral medications (such as Azithromycin) prevents aspiration pneumonia—a leading cause of mortality in acute stroke patients—while verifying allergy history and organ function prior to IV Cephalosporins (Ceftazidime) upholds the "Rights" of safe medication administration.

Critical Care Nursing Textbooks & Peer-Reviewed Research:

  • Urden, L. D., Stacy, K. M., & Lough, M. E. (Critical Care Nursing: Diagnosis and Management): Establishes that post-craniotomy patients require serial neurological checks (GCS, pupillary reactivity, and motor strength) to detect early signs of cerebral edema, vasospasm, or Cushing's triad, and emphasizes strict maintenance of Cerebral Perfusion Pressure (CPP = MAP − ICP) to salvage the ischemic penumbra without inducing surgical site hemorrhage.
  • Morton, P. G., & Fontaine, D. K. (Critical Care Nursing: A Holistic Approach): Highlights the physiological link between hyperthermia, hyperglycemia, and accelerated neuronal apoptosis following ischemic brain injury, supporting scheduled antipyretic therapy (Paracetamol) and tight glycemic monitoring.

7. Conclusion

This clinical encounter demonstrated that in emergency and critical care nursing, vigilance during routine assessments is the primary defense against catastrophic neurological decline. Combining structured primary surveys (ABCDE), rapid multidisciplinary communication, and evidence-based neuro-protective interventions directly bridges theoretical knowledge with life-saving bedside care. Moving forward into emergency and trauma practice, I will apply a proactive approach—anticipating hemodynamic and airway complications early, adhering strictly to evidence-based medication and stroke protocols, and utilizing structured reflection to continuously refine my clinical judgment under pressure.
